Today’s Objectives

1. I can find and fix run-ons, comma splices, and fragments.

2. I can organise descriptive ideas using a mind map and adverbs of degree.

3. I can write a 100-word profile in the PET article format.

Stage aim: Set clear, measurable outcomes so students know what success looks like by the end of the lesson.

Read each objective aloud. Ask students to give a thumbs-up if they feel confident about each one already. Note which objectives get the fewest thumbs — those are the priority areas to check during the wrap-up.

Let’s Review

Sentence Errors — Items 1–4

1. “My brother is very tall he plays basketball every weekend.” “My brother is very tall, and he plays basketball every weekend.” — Run-on
2. “She has long, dark hair, she likes to keep it short.” “She has long, dark hair, but she likes to keep it short.” — Comma splice
3. “My best friend loves football, and he also enjoys swimming.” Correct — comma + coordinator joins two S-V pairs.
4. “He loves listening to music, he can’t play any instruments.” “He loves listening to music, but he can’t play any instruments.” — Comma splice

Stage aim: To activate learners’ prior knowledge of compound sentence structure and error correction in preparation for the writing task.

Time: ~4 min (items 1-4). Interaction: T-Ss.

For each item: ask “Is this correct? What’s the error? How would you fix it?” Students call out. Click to reveal the inline correction. Item 1 = run-on (no punctuation), item 2 = comma splice (comma but no coordinator), item 3 = correct, item 4 = comma splice.

Sentence Errors — Items 5–7

5. “Really friendly and always helps people when they need it.” “He is really friendly and always helps people when they need it.” — Fragment (missing subject)
6. “Her favourite food is pizza she eats it almost every day.” “Her favourite food is pizza, so she eats it almost every day.” — Run-on
7. “She’s quiet at first, but when you get to know her she talks a lot.” Correct — comma + “but” joins two S-V pairs with a contrast.

Stage aim: To activate learners’ prior knowledge of compound sentence structure and error correction in preparation for the writing task.

Time: ~4 min (items 5-7). Interaction: T-Ss.

Continue open-class pattern. Item 5 = fragment (no subject — add “He is”). Item 6 = run-on (add comma + “so”). Item 7 = correct. Bridge: “These three checks — run-ons, comma splices, fragments — are what you need before submitting any writing.”

Before You Write: Make a Mind Map

A mind map is like a map before a journey — it helps you know where you’re going.

Brainstorm ideas about the person and organise them into categories before you start writing.

Stage aim: To generate and organise ideas for the writing task.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the mind map image. Explain: “The person’s name goes in the centre. Branches out from there — each branch is a category.” Connect to the Jimmy model from the coursebook p.12.

Why Make a Mind Map?

No more blank pages — ideas flow once you start writing down what you know.

See everything at once — spot connections between ideas and choose the best ones.

Better organisation — your article won’t jump randomly between topics.

Stage aim: To generate and organise ideas for the writing task.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read through each benefit. Emphasise: “A mind map saves you time in the long run. You won’t have to rewrite or reorganise later.” Then advance to the task slide.

high

“He is slightly shy” → a low amount

“He is extremely shy” → a very high amount

Point to the scale. Explain: “Adverbs of degree tell us how strong the adjective is. Slightly shy is different from extremely shy.” Tell students: “Now open your books to Exercise 5 on page 13 — you’ll classify five different adverbs by their intensity.”

Exercise 5 — Adverbs of Degree

Complete the table in your book. Classify the adverbs by intensity.

quite • a little • fairly • pretty • really

small degree → medium degree → large degree

Stage aim: To practise using adverbs of degree for precise, accurate description.

Time: 2 min. Interaction: S, then quick whole-class check.

Students complete the adverbs of degree table (Exercise 5, p.13). After the timer, quickly check answers: small degree = a little, quite; medium degree = fairly, pretty; large degree = really.

Exercise 5 — Answers

small degree a little, quite
medium degree fairly, pretty
large degree really

Check answers row by row. Small degree = a little, quite. Medium degree = fairly, pretty. Large degree = really. Ask students: “Why do we need adverbs of degree?” Elicit: they make descriptions more accurate — “shy” is different from “quite shy” or “really shy.”

PET Writing Part 2

Let’s Write Together

1. A Catchy Title

What it is: a short, interesting heading that makes the reader want to read more

Example — Rudolf Nureyev:

“A Life on Stage”

or: “The Man Who Danced” / “My Favourite Person”

Explain: “The title is the first thing the reader sees. It should be short and interesting, not a full sentence.” Show the Nureyev example. Ask students what title they might give their own article. Students copy into notebooks.

2. Engaging Opening

What it is: a question or a bold statement that hooks the reader

Example — Rudolf Nureyev:

“Have you ever seen someone who looks like they have a fascinating story? Let me tell you about Rudolf Nureyev.”

Explain: “Start with a question — it immediately involves the reader. The question should connect to the topic of your article.” Ask students to write one question for their own profile. Students copy the example into notebooks.

3. Body — One Paragraph per Question

What it is: answer each question in its own paragraph

Example — Rudolf Nureyev:

Appearance: He was quite tall with a strong jaw and high cheekbones. His eyes were very intense.

Personality: He came across as very determined and passionate. You could tell he was a perfectionist.

Interests: His main passion was ballet and he spent most of his life performing on stage.

Explain: “Use your mind map from Exercise 3. Each branch becomes one paragraph. Look at the prompt: what do they look like? (appearance), what are they like? (personality), what do they enjoy? (interests).” Students copy the structure into notebooks.

4. Closing Sentence

What it is: a final opinion or reflection that wraps up the article

Example — Rudolf Nureyev:

“All in all, Nureyev was a truly remarkable person. His dedication to his art made him unforgettable, and that is what I admire most about him.”

Explain: “End with a question — it makes the reader think. This is exactly what the examiners want to see in a PET article.” Show how the question mirrors the “Articles wanted” prompt. Students copy into notebooks.

Linking Phrases

Opening

Let me tell you about…

“Let me tell you about Rudolf Nureyev.”

First of all…

To begin with…

Adding ideas

Also…

What is more…

On top of that…

Read each phrase. Model: “Let me tell you about Rudolf Nureyev. Also, he was a famous ballet dancer.” Ask students to choose one Opening phrase and one Adding phrase for their own profile. Copy into notebooks.

Linking Phrases

Contrasting

However…

“He was famous, however he stayed modest.”

On the other hand…

Although…

Giving reasons

Because…

“He is memorable because of his passion.”

The reason is…

This is because…

Model contrasting: “He was very intense. However, he was also kind to his friends.” Model giving reasons: “He is memorable because of his incredible talent.” Students copy into notebooks.

Linking Phrases

Giving opinions

In my opinion…

“In my opinion, he was one of the best dancers.”

I think that…

From my point of view…

Closing

All in all…

To sum up…

In conclusion…

Model: “In my opinion, he was one of the most talented dancers of his time. All in all, he lived a remarkable life.” Students copy into notebooks.

Useful Phrases for Describing People

He/She comes across as…

“He comes across as very intense and passionate.”

What stands out most about him/her is…

“What stands out most about him is his elegant posture.”

He/She is the kind of person who…

“He is the kind of person who commands attention in any room.”

Read each frame and its example. Ask one student to complete the “comes across as” frame for Nureyev. Then ask students to complete the other two frames in their heads before sharing. Copy into notebooks.

More Useful Phrases

You can tell that…

“You can tell that he was very dedicated to his career.”

One thing I have always noticed is…

“One thing I have always noticed is how he never looks away from the camera.”

Model: “You can tell that he was very dedicated to his career just by looking at his intense expression.” Ask students to complete the “One thing” frame for their own person. Copy into notebooks.

First Draft — Write Your Profile

You see this notice:

Articles wanted! — DESCRIBE SOMEONE YOU KNOW

Write about someone you know well.

What do they look like? • What are they like? • What do they enjoy?

We’ll publish the best articles.

Your profile must have:

□ Catchy title

□ Engaging opening sentence

□ 3 paragraphs (appearance, personality, interests)

□ At least 3 adverbs of degree

□ At least 1 compound sentence

□ At least 2 linking phrases + 1 descriptive frame

Stage aim: To produce a first draft of a descriptive profile that applies the mind map content, adverbs of degree, and the four-part PET article structure with linking language.

Time: 12 min. Interaction: S (individual writing).

Students write their first draft (about 100 words). Remind them to check for run-ons, comma splices, and fragments as they write. Teacher circulates, offering individual support and noting common patterns for the next lesson. Do not collect drafts — students keep them for peer editing next lesson.
