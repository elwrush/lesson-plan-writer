Let’s Review

Sentence Errors — Items 1–4

1. “Many teenagers feel stressed about school they also worry about their friendships.” “Many teenagers feel stressed about school, and they also worry about their friendships.” — Run-on
2. “A good friend always listens to you, they never judge your feelings.” “A good friend always listens to you, and they never judge your feelings.” — Comma splice
3. “Really important to talk about your feelings with someone you trust.” “It is really important to talk about your feelings with someone you trust.” — Fragment (missing subject)
4. “Close friendships can reduce stress, and they can also help you feel happier.” Correct — comma + coordinator joins two S-V pairs.

Stage aim: To reactivate learners’ knowledge of fragments, run-ons, comma splices, and compound sentences.

Time: ~4 min (items 1-4). Interaction: T-Ss.

For each item: ask “Is this correct? What’s the error? How would you fix it?” Students call out. Click to reveal the inline correction. Item 1 = run-on (no conjunction), item 2 = comma splice (comma but no coordinator), item 3 = fragment (missing subject), item 4 = correct.

Sentence Errors — Items 5–7

5. “Some teens keep their problems to themselves they don’t know who to trust.” “Some teens keep their problems to themselves, so they feel more alone.” — Run-on
6. “Friends can help you see things differently, they can give you good advice.” “Friends can help you see things differently, and they can give you good advice.” — Comma splice
7. “Because a true friend always supports you no matter what.” “A true friend always supports you no matter what.” — Fragment (dependent clause)

Stage aim: To reactivate learners’ knowledge of fragments, run-ons, comma splices, and compound sentences.

Time: ~3 min (items 5-7). Interaction: T-Ss.

Continue open-class pattern. Item 5 = run-on (add comma + “so”). Item 6 = comma splice (add “and”). Item 7 = fragment (remove “Because” or add main clause). Bridge: “These three checks — run-ons, comma splices, fragments — are what you need before submitting any writing.”

Let’s Write

ARTICLES WANTED — CLOSE FRIENDSHIPS

Write an article for your school magazine about how close friendships can help teenagers.

• Why is it important to have close friends?

• How can friends support you when you feel stressed?

• What have you learned from your closest friendship?

The best articles will be published next month.

Stage aim: To introduce the writing task and familiarise learners with the PET-style article format.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read the prompt aloud. Point out the three guiding questions — students must answer all three. Explain: “This is exactly the format you will see in the PET for Schools exam.”

1. Hook

What it is: a question or personal statement that makes the reader want to keep reading

Example:

“Have you ever looked into the eyes of a scared rescue animal and wondered what it has been through?”

Stage aim: To show students what a hook looks like and how to write one for their own article.

Time: ~40 s. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read the example aloud. Explain: “A hook can be a question that makes the reader curious. For your article, you might ask about having a close friend or about feeling supported.” Students copy the structure, not the content.

2. Reasons + Examples

What it is: one reason per paragraph, supported by a short personal story

Example:

“When we adopted our dog Max from the shelter, he wouldn’t come near anyone for two weeks. He just stayed in his bed, watching. Every day I sat on the floor near him and read my book out loud. After a month, he finally rested his head on my knee.”

Stage aim: To show students how to develop a reason with a personal example.

Time: ~40 s. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read the example. Point out the three parts: situation (adopting a scared dog), action (sitting nearby every day), result (dog trusts him). Explain: “For your article, each reason about close friendships needs its own paragraph with a personal example. Use the same pattern: situation → action → result.” Students copy the pattern, not the content.

3. Conclusion

What it is: a final opinion or reflection that wraps up the article

Example:

“That experience taught me that trust takes time. You can’t force it — you just have to show up, be patient, and let it grow naturally.”

Stage aim: To show students what a strong conclusion looks like.

Time: ~40 s. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read the example. Point out the transition opener and the move from a specific event to a general reflection. Explain: “For your article, end with your final opinion about why close friendships matter. Don’t just repeat what you said — broaden it to a wider point about life.” Students copy the pattern, not the content.

Useful Linking Phrases

Giving Opinions

In my opinion…

“In my opinion, close friends help us feel less stressed.”

I believe that…

From my point of view…

Adding Ideas

Also…

“Friends listen to our problems. Also, they make us laugh.”

In addition…

Moreover…

Stage aim: To provide transferable linking language for the PET-style article.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read each phrase. Model one sentence per category: “In my opinion, close friends help us feel less stressed. Also, they make us feel understood.” Ask students to choose one opinion phrase and one adding phrase for their own article. Copy into notebooks.

Useful Linking Phrases

Contrasting

However…

“Some teens feel lonely. However, close friends can help.”

On the other hand…

Although…

Concluding

All in all…

“All in all, close friendships make a big difference in our lives.”

To sum up…

In conclusion…

Stage aim: To provide transferable linking language for the PET-style article.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Model contrasting: “Some teens feel lonely. However, close friends can help them feel better.” Model concluding: “All in all, close friendships make a big difference in our lives.” Students copy into notebooks.
