Amazing Photographs
Have pictures lost their magic?

Time: 1 min. Interaction: T-Ss. Rhetorical question to provoke thinking. Display the Afghan Girl photo — let students look before any text appears.

Objectives

  • I can identify an author’s viewpoint in a text about photography.
  • I can understand and use compound adjectives.
  • I can discuss my own opinions about pictures and their power.

Time: 1 min. Interaction: T-Ss. Read through the three objectives. “By the end of this lesson, you will be able to do these three things.”

Reading

Time: 0 min. Interaction: N/A. Phase transition — signal the shift from pre-teach vocab to the main reading task.

Reading — Author’s Viewpoint

Standard — Open your book to p. 39. Read the article and answer Exercise 2 with the questions visible.

Advanced — Read the article first without looking at the questions. Take notes on the author’s viewpoint. Then answer.

Elite — Read the article once. No notes. Identify the author’s viewpoint from memory.

Time: 4 min. Interaction: Indiv → Pairs → T-Ss. Students choose their level. Exercise 2: identify the author’s viewpoint for each of the 4 opinion pairs. Then pair check, whole-class feedback. Elicit which parts of the text confirm each answer.

Q1 — Powerful Pictures

Author’s viewpoint: a — Pictures can be very powerful

Reason: The author dedicates the opening paragraphs to listing photography’s powers — transport, emotional reach, propaganda, manipulation — but introduces doubt with “are we still under their spell?” The power is real but not absolute.

Evidence: “A photograph has the power to transport us to places where we have never been… to reach into our hearts and minds… to deceive us and make us buy things.”

Click three times: answer → reason → evidence. Ask: “Which sentence shows the author is not sure about the power anymore?” (Line 25: “are we still under their spell?”)

Q2 — Too Many Pictures

Author’s viewpoint: b — We see too many pictures

Reason: The author cites the figure of two billion pictures posted daily and says this flood makes pictures feel more stressful than wonderful. The quantity itself is the problem.

Evidence: “Approximately two billion pictures are posted every day… When we are drowning in a flood of pictures, they can become more stressful than wonderful.”

Click three times. Point to the paragraph: “What number does the author use to prove there are too many pictures?” (Two billion daily.)

Q3 — Snapshots or Serious?

Author’s viewpoint: a — We prefer snapshots to serious photography

Reason: The author describes most daily images as “everyday snapshots” — selfies, cats, families — and dismisses them as “nice, but not powerful.” This shows the author thinks our attention is on disposable content rather than meaningful photography.

Evidence: “Most of the pictures that we see on a daily basis aren’t all that wonderful anyway. They are mostly everyday snapshots … Nice, but not powerful.”

Click three times. Elicit: “What examples does the author give of everyday snapshots?” (Selfies, cats, families, friends, celebrities with new babies.)

Q4 — Media Overload

Author’s viewpoint: b — Images affect us less due to the media

Reason: The author gives two statistics — 3,500 marketing messages a day and 99% making no impact — to prove that media over-saturation has made us immune to images.

Evidence: “It has been estimated that some people see as many as 3,500 marketing messages a day… 99% of those images make little or no impact on us. We are immune to their magic.”

Click three times. Contrast with Q1: photos CAN be powerful, but the media delivers so many that most have lost their effect.
