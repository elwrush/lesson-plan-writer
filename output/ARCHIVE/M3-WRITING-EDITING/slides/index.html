What makes a good hook?

“Have you ever sat down at a piano, placed your hands on the keys, and realised you had no idea what to do?”

This hook works because:

• It is a question — makes the reader think

• Uses ‘you’ — puts the reader in the scene

• Creates a vivid image — you can picture it

• Builds curiosity — you want to read on

Let’s Review the Checklist

Get ready to write corrections

1. Capitalisation

• Sentence starts with a capital

• Proper nouns: names, places

• ‘I’ is always capitalised

✗ “i have a lesson.”

✓ “I have a lesson.”

✗ “She is from england.”

✓ “She is from England.”

✗ “My friend and i play.”

✓ “My friend and I play.”

Stage aim: To reactivate prior knowledge of capitalisation (rules 1-3) with concrete before/after examples.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read each rule. Click to reveal the example. Ask: "What changed? Why?" Point to the bordered/underlined letter.

1. Capitalisation — continued

• Common nouns — no capitals

• Apostrophes in contractions

✗ “My Mother is a big Fan.”

✓ “My mother is a big fan.”

✗ “Didnt you? Im tired.”

✓ “Didn’t you? I’m tired.”

Stage aim: To reactivate prior knowledge of capitalisation (rules 4-5) with concrete before/after examples.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Common nouns rule: middle schoolers over-capitalise 'Mother', 'Teacher', 'School' from habit. Apostrophe rule: they forget the apostrophe is part of the contraction. Emphasise: "The apostrophe is not optional — it changes the meaning."

2. Compound Sentences

• Two S-V pairs need comma + coordinator

• Comma splice? Add a coordinator

• Short sentences? Combine them

✗ “I love piano. I practise daily.”

✓ “I love piano, and I practise daily.”

✗ “I practise daily, I get better.”

✓ “I practise daily, so I get better.”

✗ “I love piano. It relaxes me.”

✓ “I love piano, and it relaxes me.”

Stage aim: To reactivate prior knowledge of compound sentences as one of three editing criteria, with concrete before/after examples.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read each rule. Click to reveal the example. Example 1: two separate sentences become one compound. Example 2: comma splice gets a coordinator added. Example 3: two simple ideas combine. Emphasise: "If your draft has no compound sentences, find two related ideas and use a comma + coordinator."

3. The Hook

• Grab the reader’s attention

• Use a question or vivid image

✗ “I think playing piano is fun.”

✓ “Have you ever tried to play piano with frozen fingers?”

✗ “My article is about piano lessons.”

✓ “Picture this: you’re ten years old and your fingers barely reach the keys.”

Stage aim: To reactivate prior knowledge of the hook as one of three editing criteria, with concrete before/after examples.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Example 1: "I think" replaced by a question with 'you'. Example 2: "My article is about" replaced by a vivid image with 'you'.

3. The Hook — continued

• Avoid “I will talk about…”

✗ “I will talk about why piano is great.”

✓ “What is the hardest thing you have ever learned to do?”

Stage aim: To reinforce the hook criterion with a third example — the "I will talk about" pattern.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Example 3: "I will talk about" replaced by a curiosity-building question. This is the most common weak hook in middle school writing. Emphasise: "Your first sentence should make the reader curious, not tell them what you are going to say."

Body Paragraphs Answer Your Hook

Your hook asks a question. Each body paragraph answers it.

Hook question

“Have you ever…?”

→

Reason 1

Answers part of the question

→

Reason 2

Answers another part

Stage aim: To remind students that their body paragraphs must directly respond to the question their hook raises.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the diagram: "Your hook asks a question. Your first body paragraph should give one reason that answers that question. Your second body paragraph gives another reason. If your hook asks 'Who matters most?' then your body paragraphs must give specific reasons — not a different topic."

Personal Examples + Transitions

Every body paragraph needs:

1. A personal example that supports your reason

2. Transition phrases to connect your ideas

Piano example

“I love playing piano because it helps me relax. For example, after a long day at school, I sit down and play for twenty minutes. It calms my mind and helps me forget about stress. In addition, my teacher says I focus better after I play.”

Stage aim: To remind students that body paragraphs need personal examples and transition phrases to be effective.

Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the model paragraph. "The yellow underlined phrases are transitions — they connect ideas. The rest of the paragraph is a personal example that supports the reason. When you check your partner's body paragraphs, look for: is there a real example? Are there transitions like 'for example' or 'also'?"

The Conclusion

A good conclusion has:

1. A concluding transition — All in all, To sum up

2. A broader reflection — not just a repeat of your reasons

Piano example

“All in all, learning piano has taught me something important. You don’t have to be perfect at something to enjoy it — you just have to keep trying. That is a lesson I will carry with me everywhere.”

Stage aim: To remind students of the conclusion structure — a transition opener followed by a broader reflection on the topic.

Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the example. "The yellow underlined phrase is the concluding transition — 'All in all'. The rest is a broader reflection that goes beyond the specific piano story to a general life lesson. The conclusion should not just repeat your two reasons. It should broaden to a wider point."
