Making your description clearer and stronger

1. I can check my partner’s draft for capitalisation mistakes.

2. I can find comma splices and suggest compound sentences.

3. I can give feedback on my partner’s hook sentence.

Which version is better? Why?

Version A

“i love FootBall. my Coach is nice. he is Helpful. i practise every Day. it is Fun.”

Version B

“Do you love playing football as much as I do? Let me tell you about my first season. My coach is really kind and he is very helpful. I practise every day, and I am getting better.”

Stage aim: To reactivate prior knowledge of capitalisation, compound sentences, and the hook. Time: ~3 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Pairs discuss. Elicit: Version A has capitalisation errors (i, FootBall, Coach, Helpful, Day, Fun), no compound sentences (all short simple sentences), weak hook (just starts with "i love"). Version B uses a question hook, comma + coordinators, and correct capitals.

What makes a good hook?

“Do you love playing football as much as I do?”

This hook works because:

• It is a question — makes the reader think

• Uses ‘you’ — puts the reader in the story

• Creates curiosity — you want to read more

Stage aim: To show students what makes a good hook effective. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read the hook aloud. Point to the right column: "This hook works for three reasons." Advance to reveal the key words underlined.

Let’s Review the Checklist

Get ready to write corrections

1. Capitalisation

• Start every sentence with a capital

• Capitalise names, places, ‘I’

• No capitals on common nouns

✗ “i love playing football.”

✓ “I love playing football.”

✗ “My coach is from spain.”

✓ “My coach is from Spain.”

✗ “My Mum is a great Cook.”

✓ “My mum is a great cook.”

Stage aim: To reactivate capitalisation rules with concrete examples. Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read each rule. Click to reveal the example. Ask: "What changed? Why?" Point to the bordered/underlined letter.

2. Compound Sentences

• Two S-V pairs need comma + coordinator

• Comma splice? Add a coordinator

• Short sentences? Combine them

✗ “I love football. I play every week.”

✓ “I love football, and I play every week.”

✗ “I practise a lot, I am getting better.”

✓ “I practise a lot, so I am getting better.”

✗ “My team lost. We played well.”

✓ “My team lost, but we played well.”

Stage aim: To reactivate compound-sentence rules with concrete examples. Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Example 1: two separate sentences become one compound with 'and'. Example 2: comma splice gets 'so'. Example 3: contrast with 'but'.

3. The Hook

• Grab the reader with a question

• Avoid “I want to write about…”

✗ “I want to write about football.”

✓ “Do you love football as much as I do?”

✗ “I will talk about my team.”

✓ “Have you ever played on a team that felt like family?”

Stage aim: To reinforce the hook criterion. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Example 1: "I want to write about" replaced by a question with 'you'. Example 2: "I will describe" replaced by 'Has someone special...'. Ask: "What does your partner's first sentence do? Does it make you curious?"

Your Article: Four Parts

Each part has one job:

Title

Short & catchy

→

Opening

Question or bold statement

→

Body

Appearance, personality, interests

→

Closing

Final thought or question

Stage aim: To remind students of the four-part article structure. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to each box: "Your title should be short and make the reader curious. Your opening hooks them with a question. Your body answers the prompt questions — one paragraph each. Your closing wraps it up with a final thought or question."

Each Paragraph Answers Your Hook

Your hook asks a question. Each body paragraph answers it.

Hook question

“Do you have someone special?”

→

Appearance

Answers: What do they look like?

→

Personality

Answers: What are they like?

→

Interests

Answers: What do they enjoy?

Stage aim: To show how body paragraphs connect to and answer the hook's question. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the diagram: "Your hook asks a question. Each body paragraph answers one part of the prompt. Appearance answers 'what do they look like?'. Personality answers 'what are they like?'. Interests answers 'what do they enjoy?'. Every sentence in each paragraph must support that answer."

Inside Each Body Paragraph

Four steps:

1. Start with your main point

2. Add personal details or examples

3. Include an adverb of degree

4. Use a linking phrase to connect

Paragraph about a place — Holiday cottage

[1] My grandparents’ cottage is my favourite place. [2] The garden is full of colourful flowers and the air always smells fresh. [3] The house is quite cosy inside and really peaceful at night. [4] Also, my grandmother cooks amazing food when we visit.

Stage aim: To show the internal structure of a body paragraph using a football example. Time: ~1.5 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Read each step. Point to the example: "Step 1 is the main point — 'very supportive'. Step 2 adds details — 'stays after practice'. Step 3 uses adverbs — 'quite patient, really encouraging'. Step 4 uses a linking phrase — 'To begin with'. Ask: 'Where are the four steps in your partner's body paragraphs?'"

The Conclusion

A good conclusion has:

1. A concluding transition — All in all, To sum up

2. A final thought or question — not a repeat of your points

Football example

“All in all, joining the football team has taught me a lot. I have learned new skills and made great friends. Who is the person that has helped you grow?”

Stage aim: To remind students of the two-part conclusion structure using a football example. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the example. "The yellow underlined phrase is the concluding transition — 'All in all'. The rest is a final thought followed by a question to the reader. The conclusion should not just repeat your three body points — it should say something broader."

Adverbs + Linking Phrases

Adverbs of degree

a little
quite
fairly
pretty
really

small degree ← → large degree

Linking phrases

Opening

To begin with, Let me tell you about…

Adding

Also, What is more, On top of that

Closing

All in all, To sum up

Stage aim: To remind students of adverbs of degree and linking phrases. Time: ~1 min. Interaction: T-Ss.

Point to the scale: "Adverbs of degree make your descriptions more accurate — 'shy' is different from 'quite shy' or 'really shy'." Point to the linking boxes: "Use at least two of these to connect your ideas. Opening phrases start a paragraph, adding phrases add more information, closing phrases end the article."

Let’s Revise

Write Your Final Version

Use your partner’s feedback to write your final profile.

1. Capital letters — check every sentence, names, ‘I’

2. At least 1 compound sentence — comma + coordinator

3. A strong hook — question or bold statement

Stage aim: To give students time to produce a clean final version. Time: 15 min. Interaction: S.

Students write/type final version. Teacher circulates: "Your partner put a ? here — what do you think they found confusing?", "No compound sentence? Where could you add one?"
