How to Build a Compound Sentence

Simple vs Compound

Simple (compound verb):

My family goes camping every summer and usually has fun.

Formula: S V V (one subject, two verbs)

Compound:

My family goes camping every summer, and we usually have fun.

Formula: S V, and S V (two subjects, two verbs)

"Simple: one subject, two actions. Compound: two subjects, two actions, comma + and."

Simple vs Compound

Simple (compound verb):

My family goes camping every summer and usually has fun.

Formula: S V V (one subject, two verbs)

Compound:

My family goes camping every summer, and we usually have fun.

Formula: S V, and S V (two subjects, two verbs)

"The second subject 'we' and verb 'have' — that's what makes it compound. Notice the comma before 'and'."

Coordinator: AND

Meaning: connects two similar ideas

"They were happy, and they were poor."

Idea 1 + and + Idea 2

"These are two similar facts about the couple. AND joins them."

Coordinator: BUT

Meaning: connects two opposite ideas

"He could choose a big box, but he finally chose a small one."

Idea 1 + but + opposite idea

"BUT shows contrast — big vs small, the opposite expectation."

Coordinators: OR and SO

OR = choice or alternative

"He could choose a big box, or he could choose a small one."

SO = reason and result

"The greedy man wanted all the gold, so he pretended to be a cat."

"OR gives a choice. SO means the first idea causes the second."

The Comma Rule

Comma BEFORE the coordinator

"My family goes camping every summer, and we usually have fun."

S V , coordinator S V

"The comma goes before AND/BUT/OR/SO, not after! Three things together: comma + coordinator = compound sentence."

Find the Coordinators

How to Find Coordinators

Step 1: Look for connector words: and, but, or, so

Step 2: Check if there is a comma before the connector.

Step 3: Count subject-verb pairs.

One pair + no comma = simple (compound verb)

Two pairs + comma + connector = compound

Model item S3: "The old man went to work in the forest and took his lunch." One subject, two verbs = simple, no comma. Then S4: "He dropped a rice ball, and it rolled into a hole." Two S-V pairs = compound, comma before and.

Practice 2A — Coordinators (1–3)

S2 but "...were happy, but they were poor." compound — comma before but (contrast)
S3 and "...went to work...and took his lunch" simple — compound verb, no comma
S4 and "...dropped a rice ball, and it rolled..." compound — comma before and (two S-V pairs)
S3 vs S4: both use 'and' but S3 is simple (compound verb), S4 is compound (two clauses).

Practice 2A — Coordinators (4–6)

S5 so "...singing so he dropped the other two" compound — comma before so (reason → result)
S9 or "...choose a big box, or he could choose a small one" compound — comma before or (choice)
S10 but "...taking a big box, but he finally chose a small one" compound — comma before but (contrast)
S5: 'so' shows result. S9: 'or' shows choice. S10: 'but' shows contrast.

Simple or Compound?

How to Decide: S or CS?

Step 1: Underline the subject(s).

Step 2: Underline the verb(s).

Step 3: Count how many subject-verb pairs you see.

One pair → Simple Sentence (SS).

Two pairs + comma + coordinator → Compound Sentence (CS).

Demo item 7 from Practice 2B on the board: "You can tie me with a longer rope, or you can build a special enclosure for me." Two S-V pairs = CS.
