How to Build a Compound Sentence

Simple vs. Compound

Simple: The gold disappeared with the mice. (one S-V pair)

Compound: The gold disappeared with the mice, so the greedy man got nothing. (two S-V pairs)

Formula: S V , and/but/or/so S V

Simple vs. Compound

Simple: The gold disappeared with the mice.

Compound: The gold disappeared with the mice, so the greedy man got nothing.

Formula: S V , so S V

Elicit: what makes a sentence compound? (Two S-V pairs, comma + coordinator). The second S-V pair is what makes it compound — the first part is also a complete sentence on its own.

Coordinator: And

Meaning: joins sentences that are alike or add information

Example: He dropped a rice ball, and it rolled into a hole.

Two similar ideas joined by and. The comma comes BEFORE the coordinator. From the folktale Omusubi Kororin (p.29).

Coordinator: But

Meaning: joins sentences that are opposite or show contrast

Example: They were happy, but they were poor.

The two ideas contrast — happy vs. poor. The comma goes before but. From the folktale.

Coordinators: Or and So

Or — joins sentences that give choices

He could choose a big box, or he could choose a small one.

So — joins sentences when the second is the result of the first

The greedy man wanted all the gold, so he pretended to be a cat.

Or = alternatives. So = result. Both examples from the folktale. Emphasise that so signals cause-effect — the second sentence happens BECAUSE of the first.

The Comma Rule

Put a comma BEFORE the coordinator in compound sentences.

The gold disappeared with the mice, so the greedy man got nothing.

Compound sentences: comma + coordinator between two S-V pairs.

Simple sentences with a compound verb = NO comma.

The old man went to work and took his lunch. (one subject, two verbs — no comma)

The comma is the most common error. Emphasise: BEFORE the coordinator, not after. Also clarify that simple sentences with compound verbs (one subject, two verbs) do NOT take a comma.

Find the Coordinators

How to Find Coordinators

Step 1: Look for connector words: and, but, or, so

Step 2: Check if there is a comma before the connector.

Step 3: Count subject-verb pairs.

One pair + comma + connector = compound sentence (two separate clauses)

One pair + no comma = simple sentence (compound verb or two objects)

Model from the folktale: S3 "The old man went to work in the forest and took his lunch." One subject (the old man), two verbs — simple. S4 "He dropped a rice ball, and it rolled into a hole." Two S-V pairs — compound. The comma before 'and' signals it's compound.

Simple or Compound?

How to Decide: Simple or Compound?

Step 1: Count the subject-verb pairs.

One S-V pair → Simple (may have compound verb)

Two S-V pairs + comma + coordinator → Compound

Remember: "I went to the forest and took my lunch." = one subject, two verbs = Simple

"He dropped a rice ball, and it rolled into a hole." = two S-V pairs = Compound

The S-V count is the most reliable test for B1 students. Emphasise that compound verb (one subject doing two things) is still a simple sentence. Model item 1 and 6 from 2B as examples.
