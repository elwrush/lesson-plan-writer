# Pedagogical Narrative — Super Recognizers (M2-4A, B1)

The slideshow is organised like this.

---

We begin with the **title slide**. A dark background anchors the lesson identity: the topic, CEFR badge (B1), teacher name, duration, and class. The logo sits at the top for institutional recognition. This slide is purely orienting — the learner sees what they will study and with whom.

Next is the **objective slide**. Four bullet points list the sub-skills we will practice: reading for gist, finding details, evaluating true/false statements, and discussing ideas. I have chosen static display (no fragments) so the learner sees the full picture from the start. This sets expectations and gives the lesson a clear roadmap.

---

We then move to the **lead-in**. Three discussion questions appear all at once: "Can you remember people's faces well? Have you ever recognised someone you only met once? Do you notice when an actor appears in different movies?" These are designed to activate the learner's own experience with face recognition before they encounter the article. I have deliberately kept all questions visible simultaneously so pairs can work through them at their own pace — no fragments, no clicks needed. The final line cues the YouTube video: "You may be a super recogniser. Let's watch this video and find out!"

The **video task slide** instructs the learner to watch the YouTube quiz titled "Are YOU a Super Recognizer?" and take brief notes to answer: "Do you think you are a super recogniser?" There is no timer — the video itself controls the pacing. This video is included because it gives learners a tangible, personal encounter with the concept — they test themselves before reading about it.

---

Next, **five vocabulary slides** introduce the pre-taught words: *prosopagnosia*, *extraordinary*, *recruit*, *remarkable*, and *scanners*. Each follows the same pattern:

1. The **phonemic script** is visible the moment the slide enters. I have placed it in yellow (`#ffdd00`) at 1.8em so learners can attempt to decode the pronunciation.
2. Simultaneously, a **hidden audio file auto-plays** the word spoken by a bespoke US midwest female voice via Inworld TTS-2. I have chosen this approach — rather than a manual teacher pronunciation or an audio player with controls — so every learner hears consistent, native-like pronunciation with correct syllable stress the instant the slide appears, without the teacher needing to pronounce unfamiliar terms like "prosopagnosia."
3. On the first click, the **English word** fades up in white bold text at 2.2em with the `.vocab-word` class styling it in yellow.
4. On the second click, a **context sentence** fades up. I have written these as two-sentence mini-stories that make the meaning obvious from context alone (e.g., "The company needs to recruit new workers. Last week they hired five people for the sales team.") — the sentences do not connect to the reading text, so the learner builds independent word knowledge.

Each vocabulary slide is self-contained. The teacher clicks through at their own pace and drills pronunciation after each word. The audio plays only once on entry and does not loop.

---

We now shift to **Stage 2: Reading for Gist**. A **red transition slide** ("Reading for Gist") signals the phase change — the background colour shift from dark to red and back to dark helps learners segment the lesson mentally.

The **strategy slide** (teal background, `class="pedagogical"`) displays the reading strategy box: the difference between reading for gist and reading for detail. I have chosen a static, fully-visible layout — no fragments — so the learner can read and refer back to it. The teal background distinguishes this as instructional content.

The **Exercise 1 task slide** shows the instruction: "Read the article quickly. Choose the correct words in the summary." A 2-minute timer runs. I have kept the instruction brief because the full exercise text is in the workbook — the slide only tells the learner what to do.

The **Exercise 1 answer slides** (4 individual slides, one per summary item) reveal each answer with the supporting text quote. I have split them into one per slide because each answer includes an evidence citation (e.g., "small — 'less than two per cent of the population'"). The teacher reveals one at a time using fragment clickthroughs, pausing to discuss each choice.

---

We shift to **Stage 3: Reading for Detail**. Another **red transition slide** ("Finding Details") signals the move from gist to detailed comprehension.

The **Exercise 2 task slide** instructs learners to read the article again and complete the true/false exercise. A 6-minute timer runs. I have grouped both Exercise 2 and Exercise 3 under this stage because the procedure text treats them as a continuous detail-reading sequence.

The **Exercise 2 answer slides** (6 individual slides, one per statement) show the T/F badge with a Font Awesome check or cross icon, the correction text for false statements, and a short why from the article. I have chosen one-per-slide so the teacher can pause on each correction — especially for the false statements (e.g., "Porritt remembers having good recognizing skills in childhood, not early adulthood") — and explain why the original statement was wrong before moving on.

The **Exercise 3 task slide** gives the instruction for the question-answering exercise with a 3-minute timer. Again, brief — the six questions are in the workbook.

The **Exercise 3 answer slides** (6 individual slides, one per question) show the question stem and the answer together. I have split these into individual slides because each answer is a full-sentence response that deserves discussion (e.g., "He has had to arrest people when off duty because he recognised them"). The teacher can elicit the learner's version first, then reveal the text-supported answer.

---

We move to **Stage 4: Reading for Inference**. The **Exercise 4 task slide** displays two open-ended questions: "Do you think you are a super recogniser? Why or why not?" and "What would be some advantages and disadvantages of being one?" There is no timer — this is a reflection, not a timed exercise. The questions appear all at once so pairs can discuss both without waiting for clicks.

---

Next, the **post-reading discussion slide** with a 3-minute timer. Three questions connect the article to the learner's own world: "Would you want to be a super recogniser? What would be the hardest part of the job? Do you think face recognition technology will replace human super recognisers?" I have included the third question about technology to push beyond the text into critical thinking. The timer keeps pairs focused.

---

We end with the **summary slide**. Four checkmark bullets recap what was learned: reading for gist and detail, what makes someone a super recogniser, how they help the police, and the new vocabulary. The dark background (`#1a1a2e`) keeps text readable. The teacher elicits each point rather than reading it verbatim.

The **end slide** ("Well Done! See you next time") signals the lesson is complete. Dark blue-gray background (`#2c3e50`) provides a visual close.
