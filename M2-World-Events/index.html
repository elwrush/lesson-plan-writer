Structured Discourse

1. Opinion — What do you think?

2. Reason — Why do you think that?

3. Story — Who? What? Where? When? Why? How?

Explain: When you speak for a minute, follow this structure. Opinion → Reason → Story/Example. Let's practice by listening to a model.

Model — The Hindenburg Disaster

I think one of the most dramatic historical events was the Hindenburg disaster of 1937. Why? Because it was one of the first times a major disaster was captured on film and shown in newsreels all over the world. For example, thousands of people watched the newsreel in movie theaters. They saw the giant airship burst into flames in just 34 seconds. Another reason is that it changed the future of travel forever. Before the Hindenburg, people believed airships were the future of long-distance travel. After the disaster, the age of airships ended completely. So this event shows us how technology can change in an instant, and how one moment can change history. When you speak, try to follow this structure: give your opinion, explain your reason, and share an example.

Play the audio model. Point out the natural structure: Opinion (most dramatic event) → Reason (captured on film) → Example (newsreels, 34 seconds) → Another reason (changed travel forever) → Conclusion. Ask students to prepare a similar 1-minute talk about a historical event.
